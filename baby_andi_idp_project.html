Integrated Design Project · Engineering

Baby ANDI

Engineering a high-fidelity nasogastric tube (NGT) training mannequin to help caregivers practise insertion technique, recognise incorrect placement, and respond to realistic clinical scenarios before performing the procedure on an infant.

★ Most Inspiring Project — iDP Showcase

Project Overview

Baby ANDI was developed in response to limitations in existing paediatric NGT training models, particularly their lack of anatomical realism, tactile feedback, and realistic patient responses. Working from stakeholder input from nurses and caregivers at KK Women’s and Children’s Hospital (KKH), the team translated clinical needs into engineering requirements for a more realistic, hands-on training system.

The final concept combines anatomically informed internal geometry, tactile material selection, force-sensitive sensing, visual indicators, and audio feedback to simulate successful and incorrect NGT insertion scenarios.

Engineering Design Process

01 · DEFINE

Translate the clinical need

Converted caregiver and healthcare-professional feedback into functional requirements for anatomical accuracy, tactile sensation, real-time feedback, usability, and safety.

02 · DEVELOP

Generate & select concepts

Explored alternative training architectures and evaluated concepts using engineering criteria such as feasibility, desirability, viability, sensor integration, durability, portability, and cost.

03 · BUILD

Prototype the system

Iterated from low-fidelity material experiments to a higher-fidelity prototype with anatomically informed geometry, sensors, electronics, and a physical mannequin assembly.

04 · TEST

Integrate & iterate

Evaluated tactile feel, material suitability, sensor sensitivity, placement logic, assembly constraints, and training feedback before refining the final system.

Key Engineering Decisions

Anatomical Modelling

Initial SolidWorks geometry was based on published dimensions for a four-month-old paediatric patient. The design process later used CT-scan / 3D-slicer methods to capture greater anatomical detail.

Material Selection

Multiple materials were explored for realism, flexibility, durability, printability, and sensor compatibility. TPU-80A was selected for the final GI tract approach as a practical balance of tactile feel and durability.

Sensing & Feedback

The prototype used four FSRs with an Arduino Nano, LEDs, and a speaker. Sensor locations represented insertion entry, nasopharyngeal misplacement, and successful progression to support real-time visual and audio feedback.

Prototype Integration

  • Force-sensitive resistors integrated into the insertion pathway.
  • LED feedback used to represent incorrect and successful placement states.
  • Audio feedback used to simulate normal crying and distress.
  • Electronics embedded into the physical mannequin assembly.
  • 3D-printed internal anatomy housed within a modified resin skull.
  • Hands-on fabrication included wiring, soldering, fitting, assembly, and finishing.

What the Project Demonstrated

The project brought together product design, mechanical prototyping, anatomical CAD, materials engineering, embedded sensing, electronics, fabrication, and stakeholder engagement in one integrated engineering system.

The outcome was not simply a model of anatomy, but a training prototype designed around how users actually learn, make mistakes, receive feedback, and build confidence.
